ZOOM 1

The whole game in one sentence

Two teams take turns. One team bats and tries to score as many runs as it can. Then they swap, and the other team tries to score more.

That's it. That's the game. Whoever has more runs at the end wins. Everything else is detail about how runs are scored and when a turn ends.

A team's turn is called an innings (always with an "s", even for one). While one team bats, the other team fields: one of them throws the ball (the bowler) and the rest spread out around the field to stop runs.

ZOOM 2

When does a turn end?

The bowler throws the ball at the batter, one ball at a time. Every ball, three things can happen: runs are scored, nothing happens, or the batter is out. A team's turn ends when it runs out of batters or runs out of balls.

ENDS IF…10 batters are outCalled "all out". Why 10 and not 11? Batters always play in pairs, so the last one has no partner.
…OR IF…The balls run outIn the popular short format (T20) each team gets 120 balls. Use them or lose them.
…OR, 2ND TEAM ONLYThe target is passedThe team batting second stops the moment it beats the first team's score.

So the whole game is a balancing act. Swing hard and you score faster, but you're more likely to get out. Play safe and you'll survive, but you might waste your balls. Try it.

ZOOM 3

How runs are scored

In the middle of the field is a strip of flat ground called the pitch, with a set of sticks (the stumps) at each end. One batter stands at each end. When the batter hits the ball, there are two ways to score.

1. Run for it

Both batters sprint to swap ends while the fielders chase the ball. Every swap = 1 run. They can keep going back and forth for 2 or 3 runs, as long as they get there before the ball comes back.

2. Hit the boundary

There's a rope around the edge of the field. Ball reaches it rolling or bouncing: 4 runs. Ball flies over it without touching the ground: 6 runs. No running needed.

Where the ball ends up decides the score. Try aiming between the fielders, then try the rope.
  • Into a gap: the fielder has to run, so there's time for runs.
  • Straight at a fielder: they stop it. Probably no run.
  • In the air near a fielder: they catch it. You're out!

That's the hidden tension you saw in Zoom 2. Hitting the ball in the air is how you get a 6, but it's also how you get caught. Hitting it along the ground is safe, but fielders can cut it off.

Notice the batter doesn't have to run. If they hit it straight to a fielder they just stay put. No penalty, it just counts as a ball used with no run (a "dot ball").

ZOOM 4

How a batter gets out

The bowler's goal is to get batters out. The batter's job is to protect the stumps behind them, and to not get caught. Five ways of getting out cover almost everything you'll ever see.

Watch

The five ways to get out

Side-on view: the bowler is on the left, the batter on the right, guarding the stumps.

Caught and bowled are the two big ones, and they're easy to spot: the ball is caught, or the stumps get knocked over. LBW is the one that causes arguments, because it relies on guessing where the ball would have gone. (On TV they show a computer prediction of it.)

The confusing word: "wicket"

This one word means three different things, which is half the reason cricket sounds like gibberish.
1. The stumpsThe three sticks with two little "bails" balanced on top. "The ball hit the wicket."
2. An out"She took 3 wickets" = she got 3 batters out. "India are 120 for 3" = 3 out.
3. The pitchThe strip in the middle. "It's a dry wicket today." Mostly said by commentators.

ZOOM 5

Overs, and why some games take five days

Balls are bowled in batches of six, called an over. After each over a different bowler takes over from the other end of the pitch. That's all an over is: a way to share the bowling and rest arms.

Try it

The number of overs is what sets the length of a game, and there are three main formats. Same rules for batting, bowling and getting out. What changes is how many balls each team gets, and how many turns.

Compare

The three formats

The big Test match idea: there's no limit on balls, so a turn only ends when 10 batters are out. To win, you have to get the other team out twice and still have more runs. If five days run out first, nobody wins: it's a draw, even if one team was miles ahead. That's why a team can "play for a draw" by just surviving.
ZOOM 6

The noise (safe to ignore)

This is the stuff that made cricket feel impossible. None of it changes the big picture: score runs, don't get out, more runs wins. Open them as you bump into them.

Bonus runs ("extras")

Wide
The bowler throws it too far from the batter to hit. The batting team gets 1 free run and the ball doesn't count, so it's bowled again.
No-ball
The bowler oversteps their line or bowls dangerously. 1 free run, bowled again, and in short formats the next ball is a free hit: the batter can't be out bowled or caught on it.
Bye and leg bye
The batters run even though the bat didn't touch the ball (it went past, or hit their body). The runs count for the team but not for the batter.

Scorecard words

Duck scored 0
A batter out without scoring. A "golden duck" is out on their very first ball.
Fifty, century 50, 100 runs
Milestones for one batter. Players raise the bat to the crowd when they reach them.
Not out the asterisk
"72*" means the batter scored 72 and was still batting when the innings ended. It's a good thing.
Maiden a quiet over
An over where the batting team scored no runs at all. A win for the bowler.
Hat-trick
A bowler gets batters out with three balls in a row. Rare and celebrated.
Run rate
Runs per over. In a chase you'll see the "required run rate": how fast they need to score from here to win.
Figures like 3/24 or 3–24
For a bowler: 3 batters out, 24 runs given away. Lower second number = better. Countries write it in different orders, annoyingly.

Field and bowling jargon

Fielding positions
Slip, gully, point, cover, mid-off, mid-on, midwicket, square leg, fine leg, third man, long on… They're just names for spots, like "left back" in football. "Silly" means very close to the batter, "deep" means near the rope. Tick "Show fielding position names" in Zoom 3 to see some.
Off side and leg side
The two halves of the field, split along the pitch. Off side is the side the batter faces when standing ready; leg side is behind their legs. It flips for left-handers.
Pace vs spin
Two kinds of bowler. Pace bowlers bowl fast (up to about 150 km/h). Spin bowlers bowl slowly but make the ball twist sideways after it bounces.
Yorker, bouncer, googly, swing, seam…
Names for different kinds of delivery. All are ways of tricking the batter. You can watch for years without knowing them.
Wicketkeeper
The fielder in gloves who crouches behind the stumps. Catches what the batter misses.

Rules in the fine print

LBW, properly
Out if the ball hits the batter's body (not the bat or glove) first and would have hit the stumps. There are extra conditions about where the ball bounced and where it hit the pad. That's why it's argued about.
DRS (the review system)
Each team can challenge a few umpire decisions per innings. Replays and ball-tracking decide. When you see a player make a "T" with their hands, that's a review.
The toss
A coin toss before the match decides which team chooses to bat or bowl first.
Bowler limits
No bowler can bowl two overs in a row. In T20, a bowler can bowl at most 4 overs; in a One-Day, 10.
Powerplay
In short formats, the first few overs only allow two fielders far out near the rope, so batters try to score fast early.
Rain, and Duckworth–Lewis–Stern
If rain shortens a limited-overs game, a formula resets the target fairly. Nobody does it in their head.
Declaration and follow-on Tests only
A Test captain can end their own innings early ("declare") to leave time to bowl the other team out. A team far behind after both first innings can be made to bat again straight away ("follow on").
Super Over
A T20 tie is settled by each team batting one extra over.
Rare ways to get out
Hit wicket (knocking your own stumps over), obstructing the field, handling the ball, timed out (taking too long to come in), hitting the ball twice.

If you remember five things

  1. Two teams take turns batting. Most runs wins.
  2. A turn ends when 10 batters are out or the balls run out.
  3. Runs come from running between the ends, or hitting the rope: 4 rolling, 6 in the air.
  4. You're out mostly by being caught or having your stumps hit.
  5. A score like 156/4 (18.2) means 156 runs, 4 out, 18 overs and 2 balls used.
