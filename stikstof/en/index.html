NITROGEN

Why an invisible gas has frozen building, farming and politics in the Netherlands — and why you hardly hear a word about it in England.

01 / 11

Nitrogen itself isn't the problem

The air around you is 78% nitrogen. That's nitrogen gas, N₂: two nitrogen atoms bound together so tightly that almost nothing can break them apart. You breathe it in and out all day. Completely harmless.

The problem is the reactive forms. When nitrogen combines with hydrogen or oxygen, you get compounds that really do something in nature. Two of them matter — and they behave in completely different ways.

Form 01 — harmless

N₂  nitrogen gas

Found in ordinary air. Does nothing, reacts with nothing, settles nowhere. This is not what the crisis is about.

Form 02 — the lead character

NH₃  ammonia

Released when dung and urine meet — in the barn, in the slurry pit, on the fields. Sharp smell. Sticks to everything. Comes back down close to the source.

Form 03 — the traveller

NOx  nitrogen oxides

Produced by combustion: cars, ships, factories, gas boilers. Stays in the air for a long time and drifts hundreds of kilometres.

That difference in travel distance is the key to the whole story.

02 / 11

Ammonia lands in your own back garden

Exhaust fumes blow away. Emissions from a barn mostly stay at home. A large share of the ammonia from a Dutch barn comes back to earth within a few kilometres — often still in the same province.

For a small, crowded country with a lot of animals, that's disastrous. You mainly pollute your own nature reserves. A large country with the same barns would spread the emissions over far more ground.

The route: from cow to heathland

The mechanism is simple, and that's exactly what makes it hard to deny. There are only five steps between a barn and a disappearing plant.

Just how full is the Netherlands?

This is the first of five reasons why the Netherlands is stuck and the United Kingdom isn't. The Netherlands has the highest livestock density in the entire European Union. Not slightly the highest — by a wide margin.

How did it get this way?

After the Hunger Winter of 1944–45, "never again hunger" became government policy, quite literally. Dutch agriculture minister Sicco Mansholt — later the architect of Europe's Common Agricultural Policy — bet on scale, technology and exports.

That plan succeeded spectacularly. With around €125 billion in agricultural exports, the Netherlands is the second-largest agricultural exporter in the world, after the United States. From an area roughly twice the size of Wales.

The hidden trick: imported feed

Dutch animals eat a large share of imported soya and grain, shipped in through Rotterdam. The feed comes from Brazilian and American fields — but the manure stays here.

The result is a country that feeds far more animals than its own land can carry. The bill sits in the slurry pit.

05 / 11

Reason two: nature sits next to the barn

Even with lots of livestock the problem would be manageable if the nature reserves were far away. But the Netherlands has no "far away".

Dutch nature consists of 162 relatively small, fragmented Natura 2000 sites, squeezed between villages, motorways and farmland. In the United Kingdom the most sensitive protected areas lie mainly in the Scottish Highlands, the Welsh mountains and along the coast — miles away from intensive livestock farming.

The Netherlands doesn't pollute more per cow. It just has almost no room between the cow and the nature.

06 / 11

Reason three: the KDW — a number with legal teeth

Every habitat type has a critical deposition value (in Dutch: kritische depositiewaarde, KDW): the amount of nitrogen that can fall on it each year without the habitat deteriorating. It's expressed in mol N per hectare per year.

Unlike most countries, the Netherlands wrote that number into law as a hard test. And it turns out that in about 118 of the 161 sites deposition is structurally above the limit. For some habitat types, as much as three times higher.

07 / 11

Reason four: 29 May 2019, the day the lock snapped shut

This is where an environmental problem becomes a crisis. The Netherlands had come up with a clever scheme: the PAS (Programma Aanpak Stikstof, the Nitrogen Action Programme). The reasoning: we promise to cut nitrogen in the future, so we may already issue permits now for new barns, roads and housing estates.

On 29 May 2019 the Council of State — the country's highest administrative court — threw that out. Granting consent on the basis of promised future gains isn't allowed under the European Habitats Directive. You have to know for certain, beforehand, that nature won't be harmed.

Overnight there was no longer a valid system for issuing permits. An estimated 18,000 projects ground to a halt: housing, road widening, wind farms, business expansions. Not because new emissions were being added, but because nobody could prove any more that they were allowed.

  1. 1945 – 1970"Never again hunger." Post-war reconstruction, land consolidation and Sicco Mansholt's policies turn the Netherlands into a farming machine.

08 / 11

Reason five: the electoral system turns it into an earthquake

This is the reason most often forgotten — and perhaps the most important one for the political difference.

The Netherlands elects its parliament by fully proportional representation: 150 seats, and with around 0.67% of the vote you're already in the House of Representatives. A new party that owns a single issue can become one of the most powerful parties within a single election. That's what BBB did in 2023.

The UK votes by constituency, first past the post. A farmers' party with 10% of the vote, spread across the country, wins exactly zero seats. Rural discontent there gets absorbed into the existing big parties — visible as grumbling, not as a shift in power.

09 / 11

But wait: the UK does have its own version

It would be too easy to say England doesn't know this problem. It knows it in a different form, and with far less noise.

England

"Nutrient neutrality"

Since 2018–2019, English councils in dozens of river catchments have been barred from approving homes that would add extra nitrogen or phosphorus to vulnerable waters. That covers the Solent, the River Wye, the Norfolk Broads and more.

An estimated 100,000 or so homes have been delayed as a result. Exactly the same legal mechanism — and it's even based on the same European ruling as the Dutch case.

Four differences that change everything

1. Water instead of air. The English problem is about run-off into rivers, not deposition from the air. Water follows a catchment; ammonia follows the wind and so hits far more sites at once.

2. Regional, not national. It affects particular catchments, not the whole country. In the Netherlands, permitting stopped everywhere.

3. Builders, not farmers. The bill landed mainly with developers. In the Netherlands it immediately became about whether family farms had a right to exist — an existential question, not a business one.

4. Ministers can change the rules. After Brexit, Westminster can amend nature law itself; and it did, with legislation that relaxes the test. The Netherlands is bound by European directives and by a national court that enforces them strictly.

The UK has the same law, fewer animals per hectare, more distance to nature — and a parliament that's allowed to adjust the rules itself.

The five locks, together

None of these five factors would cause a crisis on its own. The Netherlands has all five at once. That is the whole answer.

  1. Extremely high livestock per hectareMore than four times the British density, fed on imported feed whose manure stays behind.
  2. Ammonia doesn't travel farUnlike exhaust fumes, manure emissions come down close by. You mainly pollute your own nature.
  3. Nature and farms are neighbours162 small, fragmented sites in a small country. There's no distance to hide behind.
  4. A hard limit written into lawThe KDW isn't a guideline but a test. If you're above it, the permit is legally impossible.
  5. A court that turned the key29 May 2019: the PAS invalid, and with it the entire permitting system. That's where the crisis began.

And then a sixth: an electoral system in which the angry half of the countryside can shift power within a single election.

Where do things stand now?

As of 2026, the matter isn't resolved. In January 2025 the court ordered the State to bring half of all nitrogen-sensitive nature below the critical value by the end of 2030, with a penalty payment as the stick. The State appealed, and the government wants to push the targets back to 2035.

Meanwhile housing projects wait, farmers remain uncertain about the future of their businesses, and nature quality in many areas is still declining. It has become less an environmental problem and more a question of distribution: who gets the remaining room — the barn, the factory, the motorway or the housing estate?

If you want to remember one sentence about the difference with England: the Netherlands has the same law, but far less room in which to let that law breathe.
